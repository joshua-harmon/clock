Periods of decades to centuries are the most common kind among Sun-like stars, and also the easiest to measure, since such pairs have been followed through whole orbits since the 1800s. Earlier measurements of Sirius’s period ranged from about 49.9 years (older visual orbits) through 50.09 years (Gatewood & Gatewood 1978) to 50.13 years (Bond et al. 2017).

TIC 290061484 shows how close the levels of a triple can get and still stay stable. Its outer period is only about 14 times its inner one, and its third star orbits about 7 times farther out than the inner pair are apart. That’s near the limit: much closer than that and the third star would start pulling the inner pair apart.

Mizar’s two pairs, A and B, circle each other too, but slowly enough that the period hasn’t been measured; estimates run to thousands of years. Alcor has a faint red-dwarf companion. If Alcor is bound to Mizar, as now seems likely, that makes six stars, though those wider periods are unmeasured as well.

The TIC 290061484 values (masses, separations, about 1,520 parsecs from Earth) come from its discovery paper, and the Castor periods from the 2022 orbit study. Most other distances apart were worked out from each period and the stars’ masses using Kepler’s law. For Alpha Centauri, Algol, Polaris and Mizar A they match published orbits. For Castor’s pairs and Mizar B, treat them as approximate. Polaris’s distance from Earth is still debated (estimates range from about 320 to 450 ly). Other periods are standard catalogue values that were not re-checked when this page was written (15 October 2026).
