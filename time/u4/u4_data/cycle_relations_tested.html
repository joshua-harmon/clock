Cycle relations, tested against chance

Each row says that a whole number of one cycle is close to a whole number of another. The question is whether the match is closer than luck would give.

How the test works

If you may multiply one cycle by any whole number up to a and another by any whole number up to b, you can usually find a pair that agrees to about 1 part in a × b just by luck. The score is how many times closer the real match is than that:

  • under 3: within chance
  • 3 to 30: suggestive
  • over 30: far tighter than chance

With over thirty relations tested, one score of 10–20 could still be luck. The number 819

819 = 7 × 9 × 13, built from three key Maya numbers (13 day numbers, 9 Lords of the Night, and 7). Because of these small factors it splits many ways: 7 × 117, 9 × 91, 13 × 63, 21 × 39. Several of those factors are themselves Maya counts, and 91 = 1 + 2 + … + 13, a quarter of the 364-day computing year. 819 is also 2 + 3 + … + 40 (the sum from 1 to 40 is 820), and 1 + 2 + … + 39 = 780, which is 3 tzolkin and almost exactly the Mars cycle (779.94 days). Linden and Bricker (2023) showed that 20 cycles of 819 days (16,380 days, about 45 years, and exactly 63 tzolkin) hold a close whole number of synodic periods of every visible planet; the rows above test those fits.

The solar cycle

The sunspot cycle is not a precise clock: single cycles have lasted from about 9 to 14 years, and the long-term average is close to 11.0 years. Fits to it can only be as good as that spread allows, so a close match to one quoted value (such as 11.1) means little. A short cycle with a multiplier of 35 only has to land within about 3% to be as good as luck. The one proposed physical link with a real research programme behind it is the 11.07-year Venus–Earth–Jupiter tidal period; it remains debated.

What it shows

Real sky cycles. Earth and Venus (8 years ≈ 5 Venus cycles ≈ 13 Venus orbits) and Neptune–Pluto are far tighter than chance; the Metonic cycle and Jupiter–Saturn are clearly better than chance. These come from the solar system itself.

Where the Maya calendar meets the sky. Two of the strongest matches are Maya: the Mars cycle is almost exactly 3 tzolkin (780 days), and 3 eclipse years are almost exactly 4 tzolkin. Whatever the origin of the 260-day count, it fits Mars and the eclipse seasons remarkably well, and the Maya built their Mars and eclipse tables around exactly those fits.

Exact by design. Relations such as 2,920 = 5 × 584 = 8 × 365, the 104-year Venus Round (146 tzolkin = 104 × 365 = 65 × 584) and 9,360,000 days = 6 × 6,000 tzolkin are exact because the calendar counts were chosen to make them exact. Checked against the measured sky, the Venus Round lands within chance.

The precession equation. 25,920 × 3 × 365.2422 = 583.9069 × 152 × 320 reduces to “243 years ≈ 152 Venus cycles.” With the measured Venus period of 583.9214 days rather than 583.9069, the two sides differ by about 2 days, about what chance gives for multipliers that large. The 243-year pattern of Venus transits is real, but its closeness is ordinary; the truly notable Earth–Venus match is the 8-year one inside it.
